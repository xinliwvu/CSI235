SLIDE 10 | Valuation is a model of future cash

TEACHING NOTES

Worked example: a single certain payment of $110 in one year is worth $100 today at a 10% discount rate. With risky cash flows, both the forecasts and the discount rate are assumptions. A discounted-cash-flow model is not an oracle. Ask students what happens to value when discount rates rise, holding expected cash flows fixed. Answer: present value falls. A valuation estimate is not necessarily the price at which the market must trade.

AI & Finance · Part A11 / 40

SLIDE 11 | Returns compound, not add

TEACHING NOTES

Walk through $100 to $150 to $75. The arithmetic average of the two percentage returns is zero, but the cumulative return is minus 25%. Total stock return also includes distributions: buying for $100, ending at $108, and receiving a $2 dividend produces 10% before costs and taxes. Simple returns multiply through wealth; log returns add through time. These are arithmetic illustrations, not historical asset returns.

SLIDE 12 | What does a market chart actually show?

TEACHING NOTES

The displayed price series is synthetic. Explain open, high, low, and close using one candle, then the separate volume bars. A candle compresses activity into an interval and discards much of the order sequence. Colors in a chart are display conventions, not recommendations. AI & Finance · Part A19 / 40

SLIDE 19 | Efficient markets are a demanding benchmark

TEACHING NOTES

Keep two ideas distinct. Muth proposes model-consistent expectations for a given information set; he explicitly does not claim identical or perfect individual predictions. Market efficiency concerns the relationship between prices and an information set. Neither proposition makes realized returns deterministic. A test of abnormal performance depends on a risk model, trading feasibility, and which information was truly available at the time.

SLIDE 20 | People simplify difficult choices

TEACHING NOTES

Simon’s specific contribution is bounded rationality: decision procedures should reflect limited information and computation. Do not attribute every behavioral-finance concept on this slide to Simon. Loss aversion is connected to Kahneman and Tversky’s prospect theory, not to Simon’s 1955 paper. Anchoring is presented as a conceptual example of relying on a reference point. The point is not that people are foolish. It is that a rule should be judged against feasible alternatives and tested for the conditions under which it becomes misleading.

AI & Finance · Part A22 / 40

SLIDE 22 | A portfolio has a joint risk profile

TEACHING NOTES

Worked assumptions: asset A has expected return 8% and volatility 20%; B has expected return 4% and volatility 10%; equal weights and zero correlation. Expected return is 6%. Variance is 0.5 squared times 0.2 squared plus 0.5 squared times 0.1 squared, giving volatility about 11.18%. If correlation is one, volatility is 15%. These are assumed inputs, not expected actual investment outcomes. Covariance estimates can be unstable.
