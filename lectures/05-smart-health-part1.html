Chronic disease

Long-lasting conditions that cannot be vaccinated away or cured by a course of medication — and will not simply disappear.

Smart Health I · CSI 44503 / 40

Definition

What makes a disease chronic

Duration
Longer than three months

The conventional threshold. Heart disease, diabetes, asthma and depression all qualify.

Course
Neither prevented nor cured

Typically not preventable by vaccine, not curable by medication, and they do not resolve on their own.

Cause
Largely behavioural

Most chronic conditions can be prevented through nutrition, exercise, not smoking, and reducing stress.

Consequence
Management, not treatment

Care becomes a continuous process rather than an episode — which is precisely what makes it a sensing problem.

Many chronic conditions go undiagnosed. Others are diagnosed but never brought under control.

A century of medicine

The centre of gravity has moved twice

1900 – 1950

Infectious disease. The enemy is an organism, and the answer is a vaccine or an antibiotic.

1950 – 2000

Episodic care. The enemy is an event, and the answer is an intervention in a hospital.

2000 – 2050

Chronic care. The enemy is a trajectory, and the answer has to live in the patient’s daily life.

Demand keeps rising for three reasons at once: formerly lethal conditions have become survivable, premature mortality has fallen, and unhealthy behaviours have increased.

Smart Health I · CSI 44508 / 40

Screening

Diabetic retinopathy: a supervised-learning problem

What you need

  • Retinal fundus images, in volume
  • Grades assigned by trained ophthalmologists
  • Agreement between graders on the same image
  • Images from more than one camera and clinic

Where it breaks

  • Graders disagree, so the label is itself uncertain
  • One camera means the model may learn the camera
  • Rare severe cases are the fewest examples
  • A confident output is a referral, not a diagnosis
Engagement question

How would you train an AI model to diagnose diabetic retinopathy?

Collect fundus images, have ophthalmologists grade them, train a classifier on image–grade pairs, and validate on images from clinics and cameras the model never saw. The hard part is not the network. It is label quality, grader disagreement, and whether the system still works in the clinic that could not afford the good camera.

Self-management

Three behaviours, three sensing problems

Nutrition
Calories from a photograph

Estimating nutritional content from food images, so logging costs a snapshot rather than a diary entry.

Exercise
Movement as input

Activity tracked continuously rather than recalled at the end of the week.

Sleep
The night shift

Sleep quality and pattern measured passively — a strong predictor that patients rarely report accurately.

Each replaces self-report with measurement. Self-report is cheap, available, and systematically wrong.

Gamification

Turning the behaviour into the controller

Eating
The sensing tray

A weight sensor under a child’s tray senses eating actions, and those actions become the input to a game — colouring a cartoon character, or a penguin fishing.

Moving
PlayWall

An interactive lighting panel combining LEDs with motion sensing, touchscreen and infrared remote, so movement drives the display.

Connection with neuroscience

Why is gamification a good idea?

Because it converts a delayed, abstract reward — better health in twenty years — into an immediate, concrete one. Reward prediction error drives learning on the timescale of seconds, not decades, so behaviour change is easier to sustain when the feedback arrives inside that window.
Smart Health I · CSI 44511 / 40

Substances

Detecting a behaviour nobody wants to report

Detection
SmokeBeat

Detects smoking by tracking hand-to-mouth gestures from a wrist-worn sensor — no self-report, no honesty required.

Intervention
Cigbreak Free

Players complete tasks, progress through levels and earn rewards, with 37 behavioural change techniques built into the game.

Engagement question

How can AI help a smoker deal with cravings?

By predicting them. A craving has precursors a sensor can see — time of day, location, having just eaten, stress markers in heart-rate variability. A system that detects the pattern can intervene in the minutes before the hand-to-mouth gesture, which is the only window where an intervention is cheap.

The instrument

What a continuous glucose monitor actually does

1–5 min

between readings of glucose in the fluid under the skin

10–15 min

lag behind blood glucose — the reading is real but not current

Trends

alarms for highs and lows, plus the direction of travel

The lag is why a CGM number is not a fingerstick number, and why closed-loop control is a genuinely hard problem.

It helps prevent extremes, minimise fluctuation, support behaviour change, and prevent complications.

Smart Health I · CSI 44513 / 40

Closing the loop

From reading to dose without a human in between

Sense

CGM reports interstitial glucose every few minutes, with its known delay.

Predict

A model projects where glucose is heading, not just where it has been.

Decide

The controller computes a dose, weighing the cost of a high against the danger of a low.

Deliver

The pump acts, and the consequence appears in the next reading.

The asymmetry is the whole design problem: a high is harmful over years, a low is dangerous within minutes.

Smart Health I · CSI 44514 / 40

Delivery

Three devices, three degrees of autonomy

Reminder
Medication management

Monitors whether medicines are actually taken, improves compliance, prompts automatically and reduces errors.

Passive
Smart insulin patch

Smaller than a stamp, carrying 100+ micro-needles loaded with insulin and glucose-sensing enzymes that release when blood sugar runs high.

Biological
Artificial pancreas

A capsule of living cells that produce insulin in response to blood sugar flowing through it.

Engagement question

What is the primary challenge with organ transplants or artificial organs?

Rejection by the immune system. The artificial pancreas answers it with geometry: nano pores small enough that antibodies cannot get in, yet large enough that insulin flows out.

Measurement

The modified Rankin Scale

A single ordinal number standing in for a person’s independence. Everything downstream — trial endpoints, funding, discharge planning — rests on it.

0No symptoms at all
1No significant disability despite symptoms: able to carry out all usual duties and activities
2Slight disability: unable to carry out all previous activities, but able to look after own affairs without assistance
3Moderate disability: requires some help, but able to walk without assistance
4Moderately severe: unable to walk or attend to bodily needs without assistance
5Severe: bedridden, incontinent, requiring constant nursing care
6Patient death

Design brief

What a good rehabilitation task must do

Seven requirements. Read them as a specification for software, because that is what they are.

GROUNDEDBased on data-driven assessment that specifies the activity to be rehabilitated
ADJUSTABLEScalable from what the user can do today to the desired end-goal performance
REPETITIVEAble to be administered repeatedly and hierarchically
RESPONSIVEProvides the user with strategic feedback on the outcome of performance
QUANTIFIABLEMeasures performance and progress numerically
RELEVANTMaps to real-world, ecologically meaningful functional activity
MOTIVATINGCapable of sustaining user engagement and interaction

The gap

Why traditional rehabilitation runs out

How it works today

  • Patients practise motor skills and compensatory strategies
  • Within a clinical setting, with a therapist present
  • Skills are expected to generalise to the home
  • Progress assessed at scheduled sessions

Where it falls short

  • Labour intensive — one therapist, one patient
  • Limited in intensity and duration of repetition
  • Carryover outside the clinic is uncertain
  • What happens between sessions is invisible
In-class discussion

How could AI change the procedure of stroke rehabilitation?

The lecture names eight: personalized rehabilitation plans, AI-driven robotics and exoskeletons, computer vision for movement tracking, virtual reality, remote monitoring and tele-rehab, speech and language recovery, outcome prediction and risk stratification, and multimodal brain–computer interfaces. Notice that most attack repetition and carryover rather than accuracy.

Virtual reality

Two kinds of VR, two economics

A computer-generated simulation that lets a person interact with three-dimensional objects or environments through computer modelling.

Desktop VR

  • Everyday computers, laptops, phones, consoles
  • Non-immersive — the screen stays a screen
  • Mouse, keyboard and game controller
  • Low cost, widely used, mostly entertainment

Immersive VR

  • An artificial environment replaces the surroundings
  • Complex system of software and hardware
  • Realistic experience; the user is inside it
  • Professional applications can cost hundreds of thousands

VR drew high interest in the 1980s and 1990s and could not deliver. What changed over the last decade was CPU, memory, graphics, screens, materials — and a better understanding of the senses.

Architecture

What sits between a movement and an image

Input processor

Reads position and orientation from trackers, controllers and sensors.

Simulation processor

Advances the state of the virtual world according to those inputs.

World database

Holds the geometry, materials and behaviour the simulation draws on.

Rendering processor

Produces the visual, auditory and haptic output the user perceives.

Latency anywhere in this chain is felt as nausea — which is why the safety slide exists.

Hardware

The headset, and how it got cheap

What it does
Head-mounted display

Takes over your vision with a limited field of view, displaying the virtual environment. Quality and price range from high-end PC VR to low-end mobile VR.

How it arrived
Oculus, 2012 to now

Created by Palmer Luckey; a $2.4M Kickstarter in 2012; bought by Facebook in 2014; Rift in 2016; Rift S in 2019; Quest 2 in 2020; Meta Quest 3 in 2023.

A decade from crowdfunding to a consumer device is the reason VR rehabilitation became a plausible clinical proposal rather than a laboratory demonstration.

Motion capture

Three ways to measure a moving body

Optical
Cameras and markers

Tracks markers within a calibrated volume and streams the data into the VR system. Needs line of sight.

Magnetic
Field sensors

Gathers data from magnetic sensors. Nearby metal distorts the field and corrupts the readings.

Electro-mechanical
Sensor suits

A worn suit of sensors. Highly portable, but some designs restrict the movement they are measuring.

Engagement question

How can AI help with markerless capture — and how do you break the barrier of motion-restricting suits?

Pose estimation from ordinary video removes the markers entirely: the model infers joint positions from pixels, so the patient wears nothing. That trades millimetre accuracy for ecological validity — worse numbers, collected from someone behaving normally in their own home, which for rehabilitation is often the better trade.

The target

Activities of Daily Living

The umbrella term for the self-care tasks people perform routinely. Rehabilitation aims at these, not at range of motion for its own sake.

Basic ADL
Self-care and functional mobility

Feeding — handling utensils, chewing, swallowing. Grooming, dressing, bathing, toileting. Bed and wheelchair mobility, transfers between surfaces, ambulation.

Basic ADL
Communication

Reading and writing, using the telephone, using a computer or tablet.

Instrumental ADL
Coping with the environment

Home management — cooking and cleaning. Community living — money, shopping, public transport.

Instrumental ADL
Managing risk

Health management — medications, appointments, knowing health risks. Safety management — identifying danger, calling emergency numbers.

Systems

Three commercial systems, one sensor

SaeboVR
Virtual ADL rehabilitation

Physical and cognitive ADL challenges and games, using a Kinect camera to detect motion and register body position. Works with SaeboMas arm support and SaeboGlove for finger extension.

Jintronix
Continuous ROM assessment

Uses the Kinect for continuous range-of-motion assessment, with games designed by therapists.

Kinect

A games peripheral became clinical instrumentation because it was cheap, markerless, and good enough.

Analysis

What the skeleton data becomes

Position
Movement speed

How fast a joint travels through space during a task.

Rotation
Angle speed

The same idea applied to joint angle rather than position.

Capacity
Range of motion

The difference between the minimum and maximum angle a joint reaches — the active range, measured against its full movement potential.

These are the quantifiable measures the seven-point design brief demanded. Continuous analysis, deeper insight, and a record of every repetition rather than a note at the end of the session.

Other clinical uses

Two things VR does that a clinic cannot

Diplopia
Treating a lazy eye with a game

Amblyopia therapy delivered as play: each eye is shown a different part of the game, so winning requires using the weaker eye. Compliance is the clinical bottleneck, and a game solves it.

SnowWorld
Distracting the mind from pain

An immersive snow environment used during burn wound care. Attention is a finite resource; occupying it with something else measurably reduces perceived pain.

Neither is rehabilitation. Both work by taking over attention — which is also what makes VR risky for a patient with impaired balance.

Smart Health I · CSI 44531 / 40

Beyond motor recovery

VR for anxiety disorders

Anxiety disorders are the most common psychiatric disorders, affecting about 14.6% of the general population. Phobias are the most common of these.

Why they matter

  • Consume substantial emergency and medical resources
  • High comorbidity with other psychiatric disorders
  • Also with migraine, muscular tension, irritable bowel
  • Comorbidity worsens prognosis and quality of life

What a phobia is

  • Disproportionate fear of a specific object or situation
  • Exposure provokes immediate anxiety
  • The person knows the fear is irrational
  • They avoid it, or face it with anticipatory dread

Fear of flying, needles, heights, enclosed spaces, open spaces, social situations, driving, insects.

Smart Health I · CSI 44532 / 40

Mechanism

Why exposure works, and why avoidance does not

Avoid or escape

  • Anxiety rises sharply in the feared situation
  • Leaving makes it fall quickly
  • Relief is immediate and powerful
  • But only until the next encounter — the fear is intact

Remain in the situation

  • Anxiety rises, then falls without escape
  • The fall is the learning
  • VR starts at the lowest degree of exposure
  • The therapist monitors and controls the increase

VR gives better immersion than imagination and is easier and safer than the real thing.

It also allows response to be assessed — sweating, for instance — in a secure environment, with automatic reports, practice at home, and privacy.

VR for post-traumatic stress

62%

of soldiers returning from Iraq and Afghanistan showed significant PTSD reduction under VR therapy

9/11

Control

exposure that can be dialled up or down, repeated exactly, and stopped instantly

Autism spectrum

A diagnosis that takes five years to arrive when it could arrive in two — and a case study in moving assessment out of the clinic.

Smart Health I · CSI 44535 / 40

The problem

Why autism resists a simple test

01
Heterogeneity

A spectrum, not a category. Presentation varies so widely that a single decision rule fails.

02
The gold standard is expensive

ADOS is the reference instrument, and it is time-consuming, costly and requires trained clinicians.

03
Access is unequal

Limited healthcare resources, low awareness, stigma, and differences across gender and culture.

04
Genetic complexity

No single gene; many contributing variants with small effects.

05
Comorbidity

Overlapping conditions blur the boundary with other diagnoses.

06
A lifespan condition

Research and services have concentrated on childhood; the rest of life is under-served.

Two instruments

Behavioral imaging and neuroimaging

Behavioral imaging

  • Eye tracking — where attention goes
  • Video and audio recording of interaction
  • Motion capture of posture and gesture
  • Multimodal fusion for early diagnosis, even from a smartphone at home

Neuroimaging

  • Single-neuron recording
  • Electroencephalography (EEG)
  • Functional MRI
  • Multimodal fusion for brain structure and function

Behavioral imaging asks what the child does. Neuroimaging asks what the brain does. The neural basis of behavior is the bridge between them.

Reported result: gaze-based ASD classification above 90% accuracy, from the observation that people with ASD tend to make less eye contact with an interviewer.

The detection gap

Three years that could be recovered

60 mo

average age at which ASD is actually diagnosed

24 mo

age at which reliable diagnosis is already possible

Clinic → home

the direction of travel: multimodal audio and video moving assessment out of the clinic

Engagement question

How can multimodal data — audio plus video — facilitate ASD diagnosis?

By making screening cheap enough to happen early. Automated analysis of ADOS video, and of home video, turns a scarce clinical appointment into something a parent can contribute from a phone. The model does not replace ADOS; it decides who needs one sooner. Work at Cognoa and Stanford, Duke, WUSTL and UAlbany takes this route.

Intervention

After the diagnosis

Translation
Making sense of social mishaps

Tools that help autistic people interpret confusing or awkward social interactions after they happen — explaining the exchange rather than correcting the person.

Interaction
The social robot

Children with ASD may interact more readily with a robot partner than a human one — a partner that is predictable, patient, and does not signal impatience.

Both raise the same question: is the goal to help an autistic person navigate a world built for others, or to change the world? The tools answer differently.
