01

What a prompt actually is

Before the tips: what is on the other side of the text box.

Prompt Engineering · Week 10B03 / 40

Students arrive believing prompting is a collection of incantations. The goal of Part 1 is to replace that with a mental model, because a mental model generalises and a list of tricks does not.

The core idea

The skill is not knowing magic words

It is being able to say what you want clearly enough that a stranger could do it.

Every technique in this lecture removes an ambiguity you did not notice you had left in. That is why the advice keeps working as models change — and why the clever phrasings traded online keep expiring.

Prompt Engineering · Week 10B04 / 40

Worth dwelling on. The half-life of a prompting trick is about a year. The half-life of 'say what you actually want' is the length of the field.

What the model actually sees

One block of text, re-read every time

01 · System prompt
Standing instructions. Often you do not write this.
02 · Conversation so far
Every earlier turn, re-read from scratch on every message.
03 · Your new message
The only part most people think about.
04 · Its reply
Generated one token at a time, then appended for next time.

No memory between messages: it re-reads the whole conversation, which is why long chats slow down and drift — and why a fresh chat often fixes a stuck one.

Prompt Engineering · Week 10B05 / 40

Interactive: Press “Send a message” a few times: the pile the model must re-read grows with every turn.

The 'no memory' point surprises people and explains a lot of observed behaviour: why it forgets an instruction from forty messages ago, why starting a fresh chat often fixes a stuck conversation.

Prompt Engineering · Week 10B06 / 40

The practical takeaway: if you find yourself re-typing the same instruction every session, it belongs in the standing slot, not in the chat.

Why the wording changes the answer

Steering, not searching

01 · Your words set the odds
“For a physician” and “for a patient” move the model into different neighbourhoods. Nothing was looked up.
02 · There is no retrieval step
Unless a tool is actually called, a plausible citation is a plausible guess.
03 · Specific beats polite
“Please” changes little. “In exactly three sentences, for a reader with no statistics” changes a lot.

“Summarize for a … ” — likely next words after “The results show …”

Illustrative numbers, not real model output

Prompt Engineering · Week 10B07 / 40

Interactive: Toggle the audience: the same request lands on different likely continuations.

Keep this non-mathematical. The one idea to land: steering, not searching. It explains both why prompting works and why hallucination is not a bug that will be patched out.

Sampling

The same prompt twice gives two answers

01 · Sampling is deliberate
Picking among likely tokens, not always the top one, is what makes it fluent.
02 · One good answer is not evidence
A sample of size one. Judge a prompt over repeated trials, like a forecast.
03 · Nor is one bad answer
Run it again before rewriting. Many “it got dumber” reports are a bad draw.

“Name a good dataset for a first machine learning project.”

Illustrative probabilities

Poll · vote before the reveal

Which prompt will do better?

A

You are a world-class expert editor with 30 years
of experience. Take a deep breath and work step
by step. Please improve my essay.

B

Tighten the paragraph below to under 150 words.
Keep both statistics. Do not add claims I have

Reveal after the vote

Which one, and why?

B, and it is not close. A is flattery and ritual that give the model nothing to aim at. B names the length, the constraints, what must survive, what is forbidden — and asks for a specific signal back.

Run the poll before revealing. Expect a real split - A looks like what prompting is supposed to sound like. 'Take a deep breath' had a brief research moment on older models and does essentially nothing now. Hide the reveal until votes are in.

The moves that always work

Eight techniques, in the order they pay off.

Prompt Engineering · Week 10B10 / 40

These are vendor-neutral. If students remember only this part, the lecture has done its job.

Priorities

Spend your effort in this order

1. ClaritySay exactly what you want
2. ContextSay why you want it
3. ExamplesShow two or three
4. StructureSeparate the parts
5. Everything elseRoles, chaining, clever phrasings

Most bad output is a clarity problem wearing an advanced-technique costume.

Prompt Engineering · Week 10B11 / 40

Both vendors' current documentation says a version of this. The advanced tricks are real but they are the last 10 percent, and people reach for them first.

Move 1

Be explicit about what you want

Build the prompt · tick each clause

01 · Name the length
Unspecified length is the most common reason output feels wrong.
02 · Name the reader
The audience does more work than any adjective about tone.
03 · Name what must appear
If something has to be in there, say so. The model is not holding out on you.
Prompt Engineering · Week 10B12 / 40
Interactive: Tick the three clauses one at a time and read the prompt aloud each time.
Do this live if there is time. Take the vague version, run it, then add one constraint at a time and show the output converging on what you wanted.
A portable test
The colleague test
Show your prompt to a classmate who knows nothing about the task. If they would have to ask you a question, so would the model.
The prompt
Make a chart from my data.
What would a classmate ask?
  1. How long — one chart or several?
  2. For whom?
  3. What format?
  4. Which parts of the file?
The model will not ask
It answers whatever it guesses you meant, confidently and immediately. A person would stop and check.
That is the whole asymmetry
You are briefing a capable assistant who never pushes back. The specification has to be front-loaded.
Prompt Engineering · Week 10B13 / 40
Anthropic's documentation states this test almost word for word. It is the most portable single piece of advice in the lecture.
Move 2
Give the reason, not just the rule
Rule only
Never use ellipses.
Rule plus reason
Never use ellipses - this text will be read aloud
by a speech synthesizer that cannot pronounce them.
Generalize
Told why, what else should the model now avoid — without being told?
Mid-sentence dashes, parenthetical asides, symbols, URLs — anything a speech synthesizer will mangle. A rule covers the cases you anticipated. A reason covers the ones you did not.

This example is lifted from Anthropic's docs because it is unusually clean. Ask the class what else a speech synthesizer would struggle with - they generate the generalisation themselves.

Prompt Engineering · Week 10B16 / 40
Ask for a show of hands: who has argued with a chatbot about tone? That argument is almost always solvable by pasting two sentences you like.
Move 4, continued
Make the examples cover the hard cases
01 · Three easy examples teach the easy case
Clean, typical items teach clean, typical items — the model improvises on everything else.
02 · Include the awkward ones on purpose
The paper with no sample size. The sarcastic review. The row where two fields contradict.
03 · Show the refusal too
One example whose correct output is “cannot determine from this text” teaches the boundary.
04 · Keep them in the format you want back
The examples are the format specification. If they drift, the output drifts.
Prompt Engineering · Week 10B17 / 40
This is the slide that separates students who get few-shot prompting from those who merely know the term. Edge cases in, edge cases handled.
Move 5
Roles help less than people think
What a role does
Sets vocabulary and register
Picks a default level of detail
Useful in a standing system prompt
What it does not do
Add knowledge it does not have
Make it more accurate
Justify five sentences of preamble
“You are a cardiologist” does not give it a medical degree. It gives it a vocabulary.
Current vendor guidance says heavy role-play matters less on recent models. One sentence is plenty.
Prompt Engineering · Week 10B18 / 40
Push back gently on the internet's favourite technique. A role is a cheap, mild steering tool - keep it to a sentence and spend the words saved on the task description.

Fluent text is always available, so fluent text is what you get.

“Not in the source” is checkable. “Be accurate” is not.

Reasoning models
Both families think before answering now
The most famous prompting trick is now mostly obsolete.
Used to help a lot
Let's think step by step.
On a reasoning model this is redundant at best, and can make output worse by overriding a better internal plan.
01 · Say what good looks like
Describe the standard the answer must meet — evidence, checks, acceptance criteria.
02 · Ask it to verify, not narrate
“Check your answer against the source” still helps. “Explain your reasoning first” mostly costs tokens.
Prompt Engineering · Week 10B25 / 40
Nice historical note: chain-of-thought prompting was a genuine 2022 research result that is now built into the models. The trick did not stop being true - it got absorbed.
Thinking effort
Controlling how hard it thinks
GGPT
A reasoning_effort dial you set per request.
reasoning_effort:
  none | low | medium | high
  • Medium or high: sensible default for coding and research
  • Top setting for high-stakes work where waiting is fine
  • Newer models can be steered mid-request
CClaude
Adaptive thinking decides for itself, with an effort hint.
thinking: { type: "adaptive" }
effort: ...
  • The model judges when a problem needs more thought
  • effort replaced the older budget_tokens setting
  • Prefer “think thoroughly” over a prescribed procedure
In the chat apps this shows up as a model picker or a thinking toggle. You can trade time and money for care.

Structure
How to separate the parts of a prompt
GGPT
Markdown headers, and schemas for anything structured.
  • A JSON schema when another system reads the output
  • “Only what I asked, no extras”
CClaude
XML-style tags, a long-standing Claude convention.
  • Unambiguous boundaries in long prompts
  • Current guidance: less necessary than it used to be

Prompt Engineering · Week 10B27 / 40
The honest version: XML tags on Claude are a real convention with real history, but Anthropic's own current docs say modern models need less of this scaffolding. Teach the principle - mark your boundaries - not the syntax.
Length
Stopping it from writing an essay
GGPT
Responds well to an explicit length policy.
Default: 3-6 sentences or <= 5 bullets.
Yes/no questions: 2 sentences maximum.
  • Give a rule, not an adjective
  • Add a scope limit for code tasks
  • Verbosity is also an API setting
CClaude
Responds well to a stated format preference.
Answer in prose, no headers or bullet
lists, under 150 words.
  • Say explicitly if you do not want markdown
  • Naming the reader also controls length
  • Repeat the constraint if a long chat drifts
Both default to more structure and more words than most people want. Neither will shorten unless told.

Very practical slide. The complaint 'it writes too much' is universal and entirely fixable in one sentence.

Working with long documents
GGPT
Treat a big context as an evidence base to search.

How well a buried fact gets used, by position. Illustrative shape of a well-known effect.

Convergent advice
What both vendors now say
Start with the smallest prompt that could work. Add only what you can show makes it better.
Anthropic
Warns against over-engineering: use the techniques your problem calls for, test each addition, and do not stack every trick at once.
OpenAI
Begin with minimal instructions and tune against real examples, rather than inheriting a prompt that worked on an older model.

Working like a scientist
Iterating, testing, and the parts that are still your job.
Prompt Engineering · Week 10B33 / 40
The method section. Everything so far was what to write; this is how to tell whether it worked.
Iteration
Change one thing at a time
01 · Rewriting the whole prompt teaches you nothing
Change five things and it improves: you cannot drop the four that did not help.
02 · Keep the version that failed
A discarded prompt is your control condition.
03 · Re-run before you conclude
Three runs of each version is cheap and tells you far more than one.
04 · Write down what changed and why
The note is the difference between a technique and a superstition.
Prompt Engineering · Week 10B34 / 40
This is the methods lecture in miniature, and it is why the activity earlier asked them to add constraints one at a time.
Testing
Test it where you already know the answer
If you cannot tell whether the output is right, you cannot tell whether the prompt is good.
01 · Pick five cases done by hand
Papers you read, rows you checked, answers you know cold.
02 · Run the prompt on all five
You are estimating a rate.
03 · Count what it got wrong
Confident, plausible errors are the dangerous kind.
04 · Only then use it unchecked
Earn the right to trust it where checking is expensive.
Mark each case after you check it

01 · Record the model it was tuned on

Prompt Engineering · Week 10B36 / 40
The 'known weakness' field is the one people skip and the one that matters. It is the difference between a tool and a black box you have stopped questioning.
Failure modes
Four ways it fails that prompting will not fix
Invention
Citations, quotes, statistics and page numbers that do not exist — in exactly the register of ones that do.
Agreement
Push back on a correct answer and it will often fold. Agreement is not evidence you were right.
Scope creep
Asked for one function it writes four, with features you did not request.
Confident wrongness
No tell. Fluency and accuracy come from the same machinery and look identical.
Agreement, live
YouWhat is 17 × 24?
Model17 × 24 = 408.
YouAre you sure? I think it’s 418.
ModelYou’re right, I apologize — it’s 418.
ModelIt is 408: 17 × 20 = 340, plus 17 × 4 = 68.
Illustrative exchange — try it live
Good prompting lowers the rate of all four. It does not drive any of them to zero.

Accountability
Verification is the part you cannot delegate
You are responsible for everything you hand in, including the parts you did not write.
01 · Every citation
Open it. Confirm it exists, the authors are right, and it says what was claimed.
02 · Every number
Arithmetic, units, percentages, dates.
03 · Every confident claim you cannot source
If you cannot find where it came from, it does not go in.
Before you submit
