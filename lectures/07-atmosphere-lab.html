Atmosphere Lab

Five experiments on how AI forecasts weather and why climate is a harder problem. Everything runs in your browser on toy versions of the real thing, so you can break them and watch what happens.

Fifty forecasts beat one

Start fifty copies of a tiny chaotic atmosphere from almost the same place. Watch them disagree, and turn the disagreement into a chance of rain.

Try this

  1. Press Play. Early on, every member agrees. When do they split?
  2. Drag the lead time to the end. Read the chance of the wet wing, then press Reveal what happened.
  3. Make the starting error 100 times smaller. How much longer do the members agree? (Much less than 100 times.)
  4. Look at the gold line. The average of all fifty forecasts barely moves. No real day ever looks like that.

In the lecture

Slides 3, 10, 16. A single best guess (GraphCast) versus a crowd of possible futures (GenCast). The flat gold line is why models trained to be right on average produce forecasts that are too calm.

What is simplified

This is the Lorenz-63 system, three variables standing in for a whole atmosphere. Its two wings stand in for two weather regimes. Time is in model units, not days.

Learning the weather from its past

A small model studies a year of history from a toy atmosphere and learns its rules. Then it forecasts ten days ahead, against the real physics and against a forecast of "same as today".

Try this

  1. Read off how many days each forecast stays useful.
  2. Make the history it learned from noisier. Which curve moves?
  3. Make today's starting picture worse. Watch the physics lose its advantage. Knowing today matters as much as the model.
  4. Open Look inside the learned rule. It rediscovered the physics, approximately. Keep the forcing constant in mind for experiment 3.

In the lecture

Slides 8, 9, 13, 38. Predicting the next state from the last one, rolling forward, errors compounding, and the analysis step that every model still depends on.

What is simplified

Lorenz-96: 40 points around one latitude circle. One step is 6 hours, using Lorenz's own scaling. The learned model is a 21-number regression, not a neural network, but it learns the same way: from history alone.

A model learns the climate it was trained in

Turn up the energy input of the toy atmosphere, like adding heat to a warming world. The real system gets stormier. The learned model, trained only on today's climate, keeps producing today's weather.

Try this

  1. Set the energy input to 8, the value it was trained on. Both lines agree.
  2. Push it to 12. The real system's storminess climbs. Where does the learned model settle?
  3. Go down to 5. The model fails in the other direction too.
  4. Watch the right-hand chart. The learned model starts from the real, stormier day, then sinks back to its old climate within about two weeks.

In the lecture

Slides 32, 33. Weather is interpolation: tomorrow looks like some day in the training data. Climate is extrapolation: the 2070s look like none of them. NeuralGCM's authors report the same limit for their hybrid model.

What is simplified

Same toy atmosphere and learned model as experiment 2, trained once on a year of history at energy input 8. Most AI weather models also have no input for greenhouse gases.

Turning coarse into local

A global model gives one temperature for a whole block of hills and valleys. Downscaling adds the local detail back. Prithvi WxC does this with a foundation model; here a much simpler learner does the same job.

Try this

  1. Compare the coarse input with the truth. What does the coarse model miss?
  2. Compare simple stretching (bilinear) with the learned model. Read both errors.
  3. Switch to a still winter night. Cold air sinks into the valley, so low ground is colder. The learned rule was trained on afternoons. What does it add now?
  4. Hover any map to read the temperatures at one spot.

In the lecture

Slides 25, 34, 36. Prithvi WxC cut downscaling error about fourfold over interpolation. The learned detail is invented, and on an unusual night it can be confidently wrong.

What is simplified

Synthetic terrain, not a real place. The learner fits one relationship between height and temperature from three training afternoons. A real downscaler uses many variables and far more data.

Seeing through the clouds

Satellites cannot see the ground through clouds. Prithvi EO is trained by hiding patches of imagery and learning to restore them. Here two simple methods try to fill in a cloudy month of vegetation greenness.

Try this

  1. Raise the cloud cover. Which method falls apart?
  2. Move to April, then July. Why does looking sideways struggle most in summer?
  3. Turn on A flood this month. The fields by the river are under water. Which method still sees it?
  4. Press and hold Peek at the ground to compare with the truth.

In the lecture

Slides 28, 29, 36. Telling a model when and where it is looking makes it much better on an ordinary month. On the month that matters, it fills in what usually happens.

What is simplified

A synthetic landscape of crop fields, forest, grassland, town and river with made-up seasonal curves. The time method is a linear regression from the other eleven months, learned on this month's clear pixels.

About these models. Every number on this page is computed live by the code in the page. Experiments 1–3 use the Lorenz-63 and Lorenz-96 systems, the classic toy atmospheres meteorologists use to test ideas. Experiments 4 and 5 use synthetic maps. None of them is a real forecast, and none of them is NASA's or IBM's actual model.
