Memory and cognition

Where impairment begins, how it is staged, and why the clinic is the wrong place to look for the earliest change.

Smart Health II · CSI 44503 / 40

Anatomy of memory

Which memory goes first

Under a minute
Short-term (working) memory

This is where initial impairment appears. What you were about to do; where you put the keys; the sentence you just heard.

A lifetime
Long-term memory

Often retained until late-stage dementia. A patient can recount childhood in detail and not recall the morning.

Fractions of a second
Sensory memory

Iconic (visual), echoic (auditory), olfactory, taste-based and haptic traces that decay almost immediately.

The asymmetry is why families often notice the problem long after it started: the vivid old stories are intact, so the recent gaps read as ordinary forgetfulness.

Smart Health II · CSI 44504 / 40

Two faculties

Cognitive function and executive function

Cognitive function

  • How a person becomes aware of, perceives or comprehends ideas
  • Includes intellectual thinking, judgment and reasoning
  • Declines gradually while young, more rapidly past the sixties
  • Many medical and psychological factors influence it

Executive function

  • Controlled by frontal lobe, anterior cingulate, prefrontal cortex, basal ganglia and thalamus
  • Organization: attention, time, planning, sequencing, working memory
  • Regulation: self-control, emotion, decisions, moral reasoning
  • Impairment shows as poor planning, difficulty multitasking, emotional swings, apathy, socially inappropriate behaviour

Executive impairment is often noticed as a personality change rather than a memory problem — which is why it is misattributed for years.

Staging

Three states on the way to dementia

Subjective complaint

Problems remembering, but no cognitive or functional deficits on testing.

Mild cognitive impairment

Memory complaints AND measurable cognitive deficits — but function is preserved.

Dementia

Cognitive AND functional deficits. The most advanced state.

Functional deficit is the line between MCI and dementia — not severity of forgetting.

Plotted against time, the curve falls from normal through subjective impairment and MCI into dementia. Treatment aims to bend it, not reverse it.

Smart Health II · CSI 44506 / 40

Types

Dementia is a syndrome, not a disease

Most common
Alzheimer’s disease

Progressive neurodegeneration with increasing impairment, staged as early or mild, middle or moderate, and late or severe.

Also
Vascular, Lewy body, frontotemporal

Different pathologies producing overlapping presentations — which is why diagnosis rests on a pattern of deficits rather than a single test.

The treatment curve tells the honest story: the natural course falls steeply, current treatments bend it slightly, and the ideal — a flat line — is not yet available.

That gap between “with current treatments” and “ideal” is the whole motivation for early detection.

Smart Health II · CSI 44507 / 40

Diagnosis

What has to be true to call it dementia

The threshold
Two or more areas of cognitive function

Significant deterioration in at least two domains — memory, language, visual perception, attention, reasoning and problem solving.

The test
Severe enough to interfere with everyday activity

Loss of brain function sufficient that the person has difficulty performing normal daily tasks. Deterioration alone is not enough.

Both conditions must hold. The second is the one that puts the diagnosis in the world rather than on a test sheet.

Smart Health II · CSI 44508 / 40

Alzheimer’s disease

Seven stages, three groupings

Clinically grouped as early or mild, middle or moderate, and late or severe. The seven-stage version is what families are usually given.

1No impairment
2Very mild decline — minor memory issues
3Mild decline — others begin to notice the memory issues
4Moderate decline — simple arithmetic, short-term memory, managing finances, details of life history
5Moderately severe — dressing, recalling simple details, significant confusion
6Severe — unawareness of environment, recognising others, continence, personality change, wandering
7Very severe — nearing death; communication and swallowing fail

Risk

What you cannot change, and what you can

Fixed
Age, gender, race, genetics

Including the ApoE4 variant, and parental history.

Vascular
Midlife hypertension and hypercholesterolemia

Both midlife rather than late-life — the window for intervention is decades before symptoms.

Metabolic
Obesity and diabetes

The same conditions that dominated Part I of this lecture.

Modifiable
Stress, inflammation, sleep disturbance, healthcare neglect

Nutrition and exercise again — the list converges with chronic disease prevention.

Goals of treatment: preserve ADL function, reduce caregiver burden, enhance quality of life — by preserving cognitive and behavioural function and slowing deterioration.

Symptoms

Three losses with precise names

Language
Aphasia

Initially fluent aphasia — conversation is maintained and grammar is intact, but speech turns vague, full of "the thing". Later: mutism, echolalia.

Action
Apraxia

Inability to carry out motor activities the person could previously perform, despite intact motor function. A direct contributor to ADL loss.

Recognition
Agnosia

Inability to recognise or identify objects despite intact senses. Visual or tactile, and typically later in the illness.

Each names a gap between an intact capacity and a lost use of it — which is why they are so distressing to witness.

What a dementia assessment involves

HISTORYThorough medical, psychiatric and neurological history
FUNCTIONAre ADLs and instrumental ADLs affected? — the question that separates MCI from dementia
EXAMPhysical and neurological examination
COGNITIONInitial screening, then more detailed testing if indicated
RULE OUTLabs and imaging to exclude reversible causes
REFERNeuropsychological testing, or referral to psychiatry or neurology
Smart Health II · CSI 44512 / 40

Screening

Four instruments, different trade-offs

MMSE
Mini-Mental Status Exam

Valuable as a baseline you can track. Patients with Alzheimer’s lose about 3 points a year. Watch for false positives in those with little education, and false negatives in those with high premorbid intellectual function.

MoCA
Montreal Cognitive Assessment

Comprehensive and genuinely difficult, which is the point — it catches people with high premorbid function that the MMSE misses. Free, unlike the MMSE.

Mini-Cog
Clock plus three words

Remember three words; draw a clock face reading ten past one; recall the three words. Minutes, no equipment.

SLUMS
Saint Louis University Mental Status

Better psychometric properties than the MMSE, with scoring normed to educational level.

Engagement question

Why does a baseline score matter more than a single score?

Because the slope is the signal. A 26 means little on its own — it might be this person’s lifelong level, or a four-point fall from 30. Alzheimer’s costs roughly three MMSE points a year, so two measurements a year apart carry more information than either one alone. It is also why education confounds a single reading and not a trajectory.

Staging with a number

The course of Alzheimer’s in MMSE points

MILD 20–24Primarily memory and visuospatial deficits, with mild executive impairment
MOD. 11–20More pronounced aphasia and apraxia, loss of instrumental ADLs, increasing help needed with basic ADLs, neuropsychiatric symptoms appear
SEV. 0–10Severe language disturbance, pronounced neuropsychiatric manifestations, and neurological signs — rigidity, incontinence, dysphagia, gait disturbance

A single ordinal instrument doing an enormous amount of clinical work, which is precisely the argument for measuring something else as well.

AI in the workup

Two places models are entering

Prediction
Modelling risk from existing data

Combining clinical, genetic and imaging data to estimate who will progress — with open code, which matters for a field where reproducibility is contested.

A new window
The retina as a view of the brain

Retinal scans analysed for early signs of dementia: “Hopefully, one day we’ll be able to use AI methods on eye scans to give you an indication of your brain health.”

Note the hedge in that quotation. It is a research direction stated as one, which is the right way to present it to a class.

The central idea

Ambient assessment: move the measurement, not the patient

The clinic visit

  • A scheduled appointment, months apart
  • In an unfamiliar room, on a good day
  • Performance affected by effort and anxiety
  • A snapshot, and the patient knows they are being tested

Ambient assessment

  • Bring assessment into the home and community
  • Record events in real time, as they occur
  • Stay minimally obtrusive — in the background of daily activity
  • Record continuously

Identify meaningful change in real time by changing both the timing and the place of assessment.

Early detection compares a functional measure against a baseline over three and six years — which only works if the measurement is cheap enough to repeat.

Smart Health II · CSI 44516 / 40

A worked example

Walking speed as a cognitive measure

What is measured
Gait speed at home

Passive infrared sensors along a hallway time the walk. No appointment, no instruction, no awareness of being measured.

Why it works
Mobility tracks cognition

Changes in aspects of mobility correlate with changes in cognitive function, and may predict future cognitive decline.

Why it matters
It can be taken every day

An MMSE cannot be administered daily. A hallway can be. Frequency is what makes a trend visible before a threshold is crossed.

Engagement question

Why would you measure a motor variable to detect a cognitive change?

Because you can measure it continuously and invisibly, and cognition and mobility decline together. It is a proxy, and a good one is chosen for three properties at once: it correlates with the thing you care about, it can be captured without cooperation, and it is stable enough that a change means something. Walking speed has all three; a memory test has only the first.

Assistance

Four devices for the gap between diagnosis and care

Adherence
Context-aware medication prompting

A reminder that knows the context — not a fixed alarm at eight o’clock, but a prompt when the pill has not been taken and the person is in the kitchen.

Deterioration
Buddiband

Detects changes in typical activity levels; if activity falls significantly, it contacts the user or their carer.

Appetite
The fragrance release unit

A mains-powered system releasing three food fragrances a day, timed to the user’s mealtimes, to stimulate appetite in people with dementia.

Wandering
GPS SmartSole

A GPS tracker built into a shoe insole, or into smart socks — worn because it is part of getting dressed, not because it was remembered.

Engagement question

What do all four have in common as designs?

None of them requires the person to remember to use it — which would be asking the impaired faculty to compensate for itself. The insole is worn by getting dressed; the fragrance needs no action at all; the band and the prompt watch and initiate. That constraint is the whole design discipline of assistive technology for dementia.

Mental illness

Disturbances in thinking, mood or behaviour associated with distress or impaired functioning — and the question of what a sensor can possibly observe.

Smart Health II · CSI 44519 / 40

Definition

What mental illness is, and what causes it

What
Disturbance in thinking, mood or behaviour

Or a combination of the three, associated with distress or impaired functioning. The impairment is part of the definition.

Why
Rarely one cause

Physical illness, biochemical abnormalities in brain function, stress and environmental factors — and usually a combination contributing together.

The multi-causal picture is why a single biomarker has never arrived, and why measurement here means measuring behaviour.

Smart Health II · CSI 44520 / 40

A distinction that decides what sensors can do

Signs and symptoms

Symptom — subjective

  • What the person feels and is aware of
  • “I feel low, sad, and depressed”
  • Accessible only by asking
  • No sensor can measure it directly

Sign — objective

  • Observable behaviour the person demonstrates
  • Looks very sad; moves and speaks very slowly
  • Accessible by watching
  • This is what a sensor can capture

The more signs and symptoms a person has, the more likely it is that they have a mental illness.

Engagement question

What does this distinction imply for everything that follows?

That digital phenotyping is always inferring symptoms from signs. A phone can see slowed movement, reduced social contact, disrupted sleep; it cannot see sadness. Every mood-sensing system in this section is a bet that the observable correlates closely enough with the felt — and that bet is stronger for some disorders than others.
Smart Health II · CSI 44521 / 40

Presentation

The common signs

Agitation

Distressing symptoms leaving a person upset and restless.

Anxiety

Excessive fear — sweating, rapid breathing, agitation. Common across depression and psychotic illness.

Abnormal mood

Unusual or extreme quality, and rapid shifts — from indifference and apathy to ecstasy.

Confusion

Disoriented or incoherent — unable to hold an understandable conversation.

Incoherent speech

Rambling between unrelated topics, unable to answer questions, or not speaking at all.

Eating and sleeping

A marked change in either can itself indicate a problem.

Delusions

Strange beliefs — being persecuted, poisoned, followed, or possessing special powers.

Hallucinations

Disturbances of sight, hearing, smell, taste or touch — hearing voices, feeling things in the body.

Appearance and behaviour

Neglected or strange dress; wandering, shouting in the street. Most people with mental illness are not aggressive; some are.

The hard question

What counts as abnormal

Deviation from the norm

  • Normal defined as average for the majority
  • Abnormality becomes deviation from that majority
  • Socially unacceptable behaviour — violating unwritten rules
  • But norms change over time, and cultures differ

Maladaptivity

  • Behaviour that impairs everyday functioning
  • Causes misery and distress rather than fulfilment
  • Example: alcohol abuse harming health, work and family
  • A criterion about consequences, not conformity

Laughing is a healthy outlet. Laughing constantly, in inappropriate situations, is something else. The judgement is unavoidable — which is why automating it is a serious matter.

Smart Health II · CSI 44524 / 40

Anxiety

Five anxiety disorders, and the most common one

Phobic disorder

Irrational fear of a specific object or situation.

Panic disorder

Panic attacks.

Generalized anxiety

Excessive, unrealistic worry about life circumstances lasting more than six months. The most common anxiety disorder — and people with it often have others too.

Obsessive-compulsive

Obsessions are unwanted thoughts that recur — fear someone is breaking in. Compulsions are ritual behaviours, often checking or cleaning, repeated a dozen times before the person can relax.

Stress disorders

Physical, mental or emotional stress reactions.

Where they come from

Learning theory: a child who sees an adult react with horror to a spider learns the phobia. Biology: if one identical twin has the disorder, the other has about a 45% chance — and fears of heights and snakes helped ancestors survive.

Smart Health II · CSI 44525 / 40

Trauma and dissociation

When the mind leaves the situation

PTSD
After severe trauma

War, assault, child abuse, natural disaster. Not everyone exposed develops it. Symptoms can begin up to six months after the event and last years: flashbacks, nightmares, numbness, avoidance, difficulty sleeping. Acute stress disorder is similar but arrives sooner and lasts less.

Dissociation
A normal mechanism at an extreme

Spacing out in class, missing an exit while thinking, not hearing your name during a book — everyone dissociates. Under stress the same mechanism removes a person from the situation to reduce anxiety.

Dissociative amnesia
Losing the events

Forgetting what surrounded a traumatic event, for days or years, without head injury. Common in wartime and after natural disasters.

Depersonalization
Watching from outside

Feeling outside your own body, observing events around you — again, most often after trauma or stress.

Dissociative identity disorder — two or more personalities, each with its own voice and expression, taking control in turn — is common in children who have experienced severe abuse.

Mood disorders

Depression is the common case

6.7%

of American adults each year — more than 16 million people

9

recognised signs, from persistent low mood to recurrent thoughts of death

Bipolar

the other mood disorder — cycling between depression and mania

The signs: persistent low mood most of the day, loss of interest in all activities, significant weight change, sleeping more or less, psychomotor change, fatigue, worthlessness or guilt, reduced concentration, and recurrent thoughts of death.

Read that list again as a sensing problem. Several of those nine leave a trace a phone could see.

Digital biomarkers

What a phone can and cannot reach

Easier to sense

  • Social withdrawal — mobility and exposure sensors
  • Sadness — activity levels, entertainment choices
  • Repetitive behaviour and memory problems — activity sensors
  • Sleep disturbance — sleep quality and pattern sensors
  • Substance use — gait, balance, sweating, heart rate

Tougher

  • Personality disorders
  • Psychotic disorders
  • Eating disorders
  • Somatoform disorders
  • Anything whose definition rests on inner experience
Engagement question

Why does the easy list look the way it does?

Because every item on it is a behaviour with a physical footprint — where you go, when you sleep, how much you move. The tough list is made of conditions defined by the content of thought, and content is exactly what the sign-versus-symptom distinction says a sensor cannot reach.
Smart Health II · CSI 44528 / 40

MoodScope

Turning a phone into a mood sensor

The constraint
What it refuses to use

No audio or video sensing. No biometric instrumentation. Only low-power information the phone already collects — which makes it non-invasive and application-independent.

The signal
Usage traces

How is the user communicating — more texts when happy, more calls when angry, calling mother when sad, longer messages? Which apps — social, games, browser, music?

The logs
Six streams

Web history, phone calls, SMS, email, location and app usage.

The model
Mood as a function of usage

Mood placed on the valence–arousal plane, learned from a journaling app. 32 users aged 18 to 29, 11 of them female.

Reported result: 93% of predictions fall below the squared-error threshold that would misclassify a mood label — with accuracy improving when personalized and all-user models are combined.

Smart Health II · CSI 44529 / 40

Comparison

Three routes to mood, three costs

Audio/video and biometric

  • Very direct and fine-grained
  • Captures facial and vocal expression
  • High cost of deployment; power hungry
  • Requires skin contact; slightly invasive

Usage-trace based

  • Passive and continuous
  • Runs on hardware already in the pocket
  • Nothing to wear, nothing to charge extra
  • Indirect — inference at one remove from the mood

The design question is not which is most accurate. It is which one a person will still be using in six months.

Implementation splits across phone and cloud: usage logs and mood inputs on the device, model training in the cloud, the trained model returned for on-device inference.

Smart Health II · CSI 44531 / 40

A second engagement

What the clock drawing test reveals

The instruction

  • Draw the face of a clock
  • Place all the numbers on it
  • Now draw the hands to read ten past one
  • Two minutes, a pen, and a blank sheet

What an abnormal clock shows

  • Numbers crowded into one side — visuospatial neglect
  • Numbers out of order or repeated — planning failure
  • Hands pointing at 1 and 10 — literal, not conceptual
  • Executive function, memory and perception in one drawing
Engagement question

Why does "ten past one" catch more than asking for "one o’clock"?

Because 10 appears on the clock face and the minute hand must point at 2. Setting it correctly requires holding the instruction in working memory, resisting the literal pull of the digits, and translating a phrase into a spatial arrangement. A patient can produce a beautiful clock and still hang the hands on the numbers they heard.

>20%

The gap between the two curves is the space every technology in this section is trying to fill.

Aging in place

Why the home is the target

Medical
Detect problems early

Fall detection, dementia onset, poor adherence to a regimen, changes in sleep, physiology and cognition.

Personal
Independence

“A primary goal of many older individuals is to maintain an independent lifestyle in their own home.” Avoiding institutional care, and the depression that often accompanies it.

Economic
Earlier is cheaper

Earlier detection and treatment lower costs — achievable through constant monitoring rather than periodic visits.

The smart home aims to give an autonomous life, at home, to people who would otherwise be placed in institutions.

“Aging successfully will be difficult in homes not designed to meet changing needs and without access to appropriate technologies.”

Smart Health II · CSI 44534 / 40

Sensing the house

Instruments built into the furniture

Presence
Tracking the inhabitant

Passive infrared, RFID, ultrasonic, pressure sensors in beds and floors, contact switches on doors and cabinets.

Falls
Smart floors

Piezoelectric sensors measure pressure per floor cell. Falls are the second leading cause of unintentional-injury death at all ages — and the leading cause for elders 79 and older.

Mobility
The same floor, a second job

Changes in mobility correlate with changes in cognitive function, and may predict future decline — the walking-speed idea, built into the building.

Chemistry
Smart toilet

Analyses urinary salts and reports over the network; counts night-time visits; can measure blood sugar, weight and body fat, and take blood pressure.

Cardiac
ECG chair

Electrodes in a chair or bed capture ECG waveforms without direct skin contact, sending the signal to the home computer over Bluetooth.

Sleep
The instrumented bed

Piezoelectric transducers for heart and breathing rate, temperature sensors in the mattress, pressure sensors for movement and bed exit, and a sound recorder for snoring — with nothing worn and nothing attached.

The testbed

Georgia Tech’s Aware Home

The question
“How can our house serve us, if it knows its state and the states of its occupants?”

Funded by a 1998 Georgia Research Alliance grant and completed in 2000, as an authentic testbed for prototype development.

The building
Two identical apartments

Three bedrooms and two baths each, over a basement of research and server space. Accessible throughout — wide halls and doors, bathroom rails, push-open cabinets, easy handles.

Built for research
A house designed to be rewired

Drop ceilings, wire trays in the halls, four-inch conduit from basement to attic through wide walls, indirect lighting and low-sheen flooring.

Two storeys, two studies
Aging in place, and the family

First floor: an older adult living alone. Second floor: the "sandwich generation" — parents two thousand miles away, with an aging parent and children of their own.

Applications

Four prototypes, and what each assumes

Reassurance
Digital Family Portrait

Supports family communication and gives remote relatives peace of mind — by sharing <i>just enough</i> data. Activity from motion sensors, weather, sunrise and sunset. Not video.

Adherence
Memory Mirror

RFID tags on medications track when each was last taken, with reminders, help reading the label, multi-user tracking and a connection to a caregiver.

Interruption
Cook’s Collage

A record of the recent past to answer the question “what was I cooking?” — mitigating interruption and distraction rather than preventing them.

Access
Gesture panel

Controlling devices by gesture, for people without the dexterity to use a conventional remote.

Engagement question

“Share just enough data.” Why is that a design principle rather than a technical limit?

Because the technology could stream live video, and the designers chose not to. Enough for a daughter to know her mother is up and moving; not enough to watch her. The principle protects the dignity the whole aging-in-place argument rests on — a home that keeps you independent while placing you under surveillance has taken back what it offered.

Infrastructure

Making the sensing cheap enough to deploy

Positioning
PowerLine positioning

Low-cost indoor localisation using the electrical wiring already in the walls, with a calibration step — no new sensor network to install.

Appliances
Power event detection

Detecting the use of appliances and light switches from a single plug-in module, by recognising each device’s electrical signature.

Activity
Vision for characterisation

High-resolution motion data from cameras, giving more accurate information and a better understanding of the activity.

The economic argument matters as much as the technical one: a system that needs a retrofit will reach the homes that can afford a retrofit.

Summary

Six things AI does for smart health

DIAGNOSEAnalyse records, images and sensor output to find clinical patterns that support earlier and more precise detection
PERSONALIZEDrive medical decisions that are increasingly targeted, improving outcomes and satisfaction
TAILORCombine histories, genomics and lifestyle to fit the therapeutic intervention to the patient
REACHStrengthen remote monitoring and consultation, extending access to underserved regions
ANTICIPATEAnalyse vitals and behaviour in real time to predict risk while there is still time to act
FUSEIntegrate imaging, labs and wearables into one view of a patient rather than several partial ones

Every one of those six rests on measurement that is continuous, unobtrusive, and trusted — which is what the two halves of this lecture have been about.
